# A Spanish edition with its own source of truth

Most visitors arrive from LinkedIn already knowing the name, and some of them work at Spanish companies where reading Spanish is more comfortable than reading English. We decided to publish a Spanish edition of the site at `/es`, written from a Spanish CV rather than translated from the English site, so that both editions sit the same distance from a source of truth (ADR 0001) rather than one being a derivative of the other.

## Considered options

**A translation layer over `content.ts`, machine-generated, marked on the page as unreviewed.** Rejected twice over. The site is a credibility anchor: its whole job is to satisfy a reader that the person is real and senior. A notice telling that reader, in Spanish, that the page they are on is the unchecked copy hedges the credibility of the artefact in front of the only person it was added for, and sends them back to the English page the Spanish one existed to spare them. It is also implausible on its face, because Fran is a native Spanish speaker from Zaragoza, so an unreviewed Spanish page implies an absence of attention that is not real and that costs fifteen minutes to remove.

**A client-side language toggle on the single existing URL.** Rejected because the first Spanish-language impression is not the page, it is the preview card a scraper renders, and per ADR 0003 a scraper fetches the document and stops without running React. A toggle is invisible to it, so a Spanish reader arriving from LinkedIn sees an English card and an English page and has to go looking for the language control after the moment has passed. It also gives up a shareable `/es` URL, correct `lang` for assistive technology, and indexing of the Spanish text.

**An English-only CV download on `/es`.** Rejected as the same warmth failure the edition exists to avoid.

**Detecting `navigator.language` and redirecting on first visit.** Rejected because browser language predicts interface preference, not reading preference, and the two come apart hardest in precisely the target population: the Spanish recruiter is plausibly on a work laptop set to `en-US`, and plenty of people who want the English page browse in `es-ES`. GitHub Pages cannot negotiate server-side either, so this would be a JavaScript redirect firing after paint, fighting the back button and capable of sending a crawler somewhere unintended. The URL is the only thing that decides which edition a visitor gets.

## Consequences

- **The Spanish edition is condensed from the Spanish CV, not from `src/content.ts`.** Job titles, section names and the identity line take the wording the Spanish CV already settled ("Ingeniero de Software Principal", "Reconocimientos y premios", and the *Resumen* paragraph). Inventing a second Spanish vocabulary alongside the CV's would put the two documents a recruiter is most likely to read side by side into disagreement.

- **This reopens ADR 0003, and the answer is a twin document.** That ADR held that static metadata was affordable *because* the site was one page, and named a second route as the thing that would force the question. `/es` is that second route. It gets its own built HTML document with its own static title, description and Open Graph tags in Spanish, plus `hreflang` alternates between the two. Component-rendered metadata is still not an option; the reasoning in ADR 0003 is unchanged, it now simply applies twice, which doubles the drift surface that the guard tests in `src/App.test.tsx` have to cover.

- **Both CVs are published, and `/es` offers both.** The Spanish PDF is the primary download; the English PDF sits beside it labelled as the original, because a recruiter often needs an English artefact to forward into an ATS or to an international hiring committee. That second button is a service, not a disclaimer.

- **Neither CV ships without the other.** The English CV is the authority for facts and the Spanish one is a translation of it, so regenerating one alone lets `/es` assert things that stopped being true, with no test able to catch it because a PDF's text is not in the build. The rule is that both are regenerated and published together. We considered a test that extracts text from both PDFs and asserts the site strings appear in the matching one, and rejected it: the site strings are condensations rather than quotations, so the test would be a fuzzy matcher failing on line breaks and ligatures, which is heavy machinery guarding a file that changes about twice a year.

- **Each edition has its own share image.** `tools/assets/` grows a Spanish source file rendered by the same command, so the guard matrix goes from three copies of the identity line to six. This is the largest single piece of work in the feature and it is for an image few visitors consciously look at, but the reasoning that bought two documents was that the first Spanish-language impression is the preview card. An English image under a Spanish title is a half-done localisation in the frame where it shows most, and a card with no image at all collapses to a link stub that reads as a dead page.

- **Strings live in `content.en.ts` and `content.es.ts` behind one shared type, chosen at build time.** Each entry document boots the app with one of them, so the other edition's strings are not in the bundle and a missing section fails `tsc -b`. A single file pairing every string as `{ en, es }` was rejected because it asserts a key-by-key correspondence that the decision above denies: condensed from different CVs, the Spanish bullets may legitimately split, merge or land differently, and the first instinct on seeing a half-filled pair is to machine-fill it, which is the rejected translation layer returning through the data model. An i18n library was rejected for the same reason plus a practical one: nothing here needs pluralisation, interpolation or catalogue loading, and the library's natural grain is runtime switching, which contradicts an edition being a document rather than a mode.

- **No freshness or staleness note appears anywhere in the UI.** "The English one is probably more up to date" tells a reader that two versions of the CV exist and that Fran is unsure which is current, which is a worse admission than the one it guards against. If the Spanish CV can lag, the fix is the rule above, not a warning. Labelling the English PDF as the **original** is a fact about provenance and stays; anything about which is fresher is a confession about maintenance and does not.

  A decision this easy to reverse out of politeness needs a guard, so `src/App.test.tsx` bans the hedging vocabulary in both languages, over the edition table, against what a visitor reads, what a scraper reads and what the share image says. Writing it taught two things the reasoning above did not anticipate. First, the vocabulary splits in two: some of it is a hedge wherever it appears, but "outdated", "beta" and "más completa" are also the ordinary words of a CV bullet, so those only count when they are said about a version of the site, and matching them bare would eventually fail a true piece of evidence under a comment telling its author to go and read this ADR. Second, the guard reads the entry document parsed rather than as raw text, unlike ADR 0003's metadata seam — the documents explain these decisions in HTML comments, and a comment defending this paragraph would naturally be written in the words the guard bans, so scanning the raw file would make the guard trip over its own reasoning.
